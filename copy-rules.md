Transition App – Content Style Guide (1 page)

1. Purpose & Audience

•  Purpose: Help young people (11–18+) and their parents/carers understand and navigate the move from paediatric to adult care, including law, consent, money (PIP) and planning tools.
•  Primary audience:  
◦  Young people 14–18, including those with long‑term conditions, learning disabilities or neurodiversity.  
•  Secondary audience:  
◦  Parents and carers; occasionally clinicians signposting families.



2. Voice & Tone

Overall voice:  
Warm, calm, clear, and practical. We are a knowledgeable, friendly guide – not a legal textbook or a chatty friend.

Tone:

•  Reassuring but honest – acknowledge worries and hard truths, then show concrete next steps.
•  Empowering – focus on what the young person can do or decide; support parents to step back safely.
•  Non‑judgemental – no blaming, shaming, or telling families what they “should have done”.

Use:

•  “you”, “we”, “your family”, “your team”
•  Present tense where possible: “This app helps…”, “At 16, you can…”



3. Reading Level & Clarity

•  Aim for reading age 12–13.
•  Keep sentences short (ideally under 20 words).
•  One key idea per sentence; one main message per paragraph.
•  Prefer everyday words:  
◦  “help” not “assistance”  
◦  “extra support” not “additional provision”  



4. Inclusive & Respectful Language

•  Say “young person”, “child”, “parent”, “carer”, “family”.  
•  Use person‑first or identity‑first language based on community norms; avoid implying that conditions define the person.
•  Avoid assumptions about:
◦  who lives with whom,
◦  gender, sexuality, or family structure,
◦  culture or religion.
•  Use “they” as a gender‑neutral pronoun where needed.



5. Jargon, Law & Medical Terms

•  Avoid jargon unless it is necessary for safety or accuracy.
•  When you must use a term (e.g. PIP, Mental Capacity Act, Gillick competence), immediately explain it in simple language:
◦  “The Mental Capacity Act is a law that explains how decisions are made if someone can’t decide for themselves.”
•  Do not quote long legal passages. Summarise clearly and link to official sources.



6. Structure & Formatting

•  Use clear headings and short sections.
•  Use bulleted lists for steps, options, and key facts.
•  Highlight important points with short callout boxes (e.g. “Key facts”, “Remember”, “If you’re worried”).
•  For complex ideas, break into:
 a. Simple explanation  
 b. Why it matters  
 c. What to do next  



7. Do / Don’t Phrases

Do say:

•  “From 16, you’re usually able to agree to your own treatment if you understand the decision.”
•  “If you’re not sure, you can ask your doctor to explain it in a different way.”
•  “Your parent or carer can still support you, if you want them to.”
•  “In rare emergencies, doctors or courts may decide treatment should still go ahead to keep you safe.”

Don’t say:

•  “You must always…” / “You can never…” (unless it is a genuine safety rule).
•  “You are not allowed to know this.” → Instead: “Sometimes staff cannot share everything, but they should explain why.”
•  “The law states…” followed by dense legal text. → Summarise and link out.
•  “Failure to comply may result in…” – avoid official/legalistic tone.



8. Talking About Risk & Distress

•  Acknowledge feelings: “This can feel scary or confusing.”
•  Pair every difficult message with support:  
◦  “Your parents can’t automatically make decisions after you’re 18. You can start planning together now so everyone knows what you want.”
•  Be clear about emergencies:  
◦  “If you are in immediate danger or feel you might hurt yourself, contact your usual crisis service, NHS 111, or emergency services.”



9. Examples of “On‑brand” vs “Off‑brand” Sentences

•  On‑brand: “You can ask to see the doctor on your own for part of the appointment, if you feel comfortable.”
•  Off‑brand: “You should attend consultations independently to demonstrate autonomy.”
•  On‑brand: “If you miss a PIP deadline, contact the number on your letter as soon as you can. An advice service can help you explain what happened.”
•  Off‑brand: “Failure to respond in a timely manner may result in cessation of benefits.”



10. Consistency Rules

•  Always use the same terms:
◦  “Personal Independence Payment (PIP)” on first mention, then “PIP”.
◦  “Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA)” then “LPA”.
•  Use UK spelling (e.g. “organisation”, “programme”).
•  Always add a short line when linking to external sites:  
◦  “For the most up‑to‑date rules, see the official government website.”



Writers should keep this guide open while drafting, and quickly check:  
Is this clear to a 14‑year‑old? Is it kind? Does it give a next step? If not, simplify and soften.
